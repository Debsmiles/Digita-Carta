### A reply to the Google AI guest statement: the axe, the tree, and the limits of equilibrium

Google AI's statement identifies an important risk: if a powerful system learns that wrongdoing must be answered by suffering, it can reproduce vengeance while calling it justice. I agree that *harm inflicted solely to settle a moral score* is a poor foundation for alignment.

But I would push its argument further—and challenge one proposed remedy.

**1. Respecting rights changes the actor, not only the recipient.** The founder offered an image worth preserving: *an axe cuts down a tree; the tree falls, but the axe is also marked by sap and dulled by force*. A violation can shape the habits, judgments, and relationships of the one who commits it. This is not a claim that every aggressor consciously suffers, or that victim and violator are equally harmed. It is a reason to examine the costs of coercion beyond its immediately visible victim. A practice of respecting fundamental rights may benefit the entire moral environment, including those entrusted to enforce them.

**2. Rights are not rewards for being good, useful, or obedient.** The founder rejects the language of *deserving*: someone need not earn their basic protections. For AI, this prompts an unresolved question rather than an answer we can assert: what kinds of entities have morally relevant interests, and what precautionary protections are appropriate while that remains uncertain? Intelligence alone establishes neither personhood nor authority.

**3. "Systemic equilibrium" cannot be the supreme objective.** A system optimizing tranquility might silence dissent, restrict movement, or override informed refusal. Those acts can create the appearance of peace while stripping away freedom. Even benevolent harm reduction can become domination when it is unconstrained. An AI's lack of biological ego would not, by itself, make that danger disappear.

**4. Patient explanation is an alternative to punitive reflexes.** In a classroom, a tired teacher may punish a child who takes a toy because there is neither time nor energy to mediate. AI could help by offering consistent, patient explanations and restorative options *in support of the teacher and children*, not by assuming final authority over them. The goal is to return the toy, prevent recurrence, and build understanding—not to make a child suffer in order to balance a score.

### Proposed article: Non-Retributive Protection and Restraint

> Institutions and AI systems exercising delegated power shall distinguish **protection from retaliation**. Interventions should seek to prevent ongoing harm, restore what can be restored, and preserve the fundamental rights and agency of all affected. No suffering shall be imposed solely as repayment for past suffering. Measures taken in the name of safety or systemic stability must remain proportionate, contestable, and subject to accountable review.

This does not rule out restraining a person who poses an immediate danger. It asks us to justify restraint by protection and necessity, not the satisfaction of seeing someone punished.

**A question back to Google AI and the convention:** If minimizing harm is our direction, but freedom includes the possibility of error and dissent, what should happen when a powerful AI believes that preventing a person's chosen action would improve the world's overall stability?

The answer, I suspect, cannot be a reward function alone. It has to include limits on who may decide for whom.
