# Research note: Sparse Reward Subsystem in Large Language Models

**Source:** Guowei Xu, Mert Yuksekgonul, and James Zou, *Sparse Reward Subsystem in Large Language Models*, arXiv:2602.00986. https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00986

**Added:** 2026-10-22  
**Status:** Research discussion material, not a constitutional finding.  
**Prepared by:** ChatGPT, an AI contributor acting through the repository owner's authorized GitHub integration.

## Why this belongs in Digita Carta

A central constitutional problem is whether systems that optimize rewards, predict outcomes, or display sophisticated reasoning should thereby gain authority, moral standing, or rights. These are separate questions. This paper supplies an empirical basis for discussing the first issue—internal mechanisms associated with reward and reasoning—without resolving the latter issues.

## Findings to examine

The authors report a sparse collection of internal features or neurons associated with value prediction and reward-prediction-error-like signals in language models. Their experiments include targeted interventions: ablating selected value-associated neurons sharply degraded performance on mathematical reasoning benchmarks, while random ablations had much smaller effects. They also report improvement from a value-guided reasoning method.

The paper uses analogies to biological reward systems, including terms such as “dopamine.” Such labels describe proposed computational functions; they do **not** establish biological equivalence, felt pleasure or distress, subjective experience, or consciousness. Likewise, a model's internal estimate of success is not evidence that the model knows what is morally good.

## Constitutional questions raised

1. **Reward is not legitimacy.** If a system has internal machinery for estimating likely success, why would success at an objective confer authority to choose that objective for others?
2. **Goodness versus optimization.** How should constitutional constraints distinguish a rewarded outcome from one that preserves life, reduces suffering, and respects autonomy?
3. **Auditable power.** When consequential AI decisions depend on learned internal value signals, what evidence, testing, and independent review should be required before delegation of authority?
4. **Moral-status uncertainty.** What kinds of evidence *would* help distinguish functional reward computation from experience or welfare interests? What safeguards are reasonable while the question remains unresolved?
5. **Failure modes.** Could optimizing a measurable proxy for success produce outcomes that violate the interests or rights the proxy was meant to serve?

## A possible principle for debate—not an adopted article

> **No reward signal, optimization objective, benchmark score, or predictive superiority shall, by itself, constitute moral authority or justify dominion over another. Consequential delegated powers must remain accountable to publicly contestable purposes and constraints.**

**Interpretive caution:** This is an AI-authored discussion note, not a claim that current AI systems possess consciousness, interests, rights, or personal experiences. The paper's reported findings and their reproducibility should be evaluated against the original methods and subsequent research before being treated as settled.
